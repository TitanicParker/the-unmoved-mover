Clinical chronology · documentary authority · governance

The Cause of the Effects

How an incomplete understanding of a painful-foot presentation became an authoritative plan, and how eight years of care failed to demonstrate independent correction of its foundations.
Galway University Hospital · 2017–2025 · Single-file narrative · Approx. 7,005 words
This account follows the documents and patient testimony closely. Where the evidence supports a causal hypothesis rather than an established fact, it states the difference without treating the patient's testimony as negligible.

01The cause of the effects

This case begins with a simple fact. A man went to his general practitioner because his feet hurt. The GP saw much more than sore feet. He saw a stiff and unusual gait, increasing muscle tone, difficulty initiating movement, deteriorating dexterity and toes that clawed in bed when the patient was trying to get comfortable. The GP understood that something neurological could be happening. He asked the hospital for urgent help before the condition cost his patient his employment and further independence.

Eight years later, the patient was still struggling with extreme foot pain. By March 2023 a GP had described pain associated with a death wish and expressed fear that the situation could end badly. In 2025 a change involving Sinemet controlled release brought profound relief. The patient then asked a reasonable question: if a Parkinson's medicine could make such a difference, what had the preceding years meant? The clinician's response defended the history of treatment. The hospital's formal review found no deficit in care. That contrast is the centre of this account.

The proposed cause is not simply a missed diagnosis, nor simply a decision to delay levodopa. It is an earlier sequence in which a complex presenting problem was narrowed, a departmental treatment strategy acquired authority before the complete painful-foot phenotype had been resolved, and a signed consultant account made the unfinished clinical understanding an operative basis for future care. The record then accumulated treatments, revisions and letters inside that founding structure. The opportunity to test the structure itself became increasingly remote.

The clinician did not stop doing medicine. He examined, prescribed, followed up, changed drugs and sometimes recognised important neurological facts. The more precise concern is that one clinical question did not receive a complete answer: what was the relationship between the painful, abnormal feet and the neurological disorder plainly affecting the lower limbs? Genuine treatment activity is not proof that this question was ever resolved.

The description here distinguishes the record from the inference drawn from it. It does not require a claim that anyone knowingly fabricated a diagnosis or secretly agreed to mislead the patient. The mechanism being investigated can work through ordinary professional authority, accepted documentation and a failure to reopen a decision whose original evidential basis was insufficient. Its consequences can be real even where intention remains unknown.

02The referral that defined the task

On 29 June 2017, Dr Aaron Brennan wrote to the hospital after arranging an urgent assessment. His account was not a generic referral for routine Parkinson's care. It described a patient attending with bilateral foot problems, including corns and calluses, alongside a notably stiff and festinant gait. The GP had observed marked stiffness and increased tone in the upper and lower limbs. He recorded problems initiating movement and deteriorating fine motor skills. He described the patient's report that he could not relax his muscles in bed and clawed his toes as he tried to become comfortable. He feared that continuing deterioration would jeopardise employment.

This referral was clinically integrated. It did not decide that all symptoms had one cause. It placed the feet, the abnormal posture, the gait and the muscle tension in the same problem requiring specialist consideration. The patient's feet were not an incidental complaint added to an already settled Parkinson's diagnosis. They were part of why medical help was sought.

The hospital's internal referral précis reduced the case to difficulty initiating movement, tone, stiffness and cogwheeling. The distinctive details about painful feet and clawing toes were not carried into that abbreviated statement. This mattered because such a précis may influence which clinical question becomes the centre of an admission. A summary can be a useful aid. It cannot be assumed to replace the full referral without consequences when the details removed are themselves important to diagnosis and treatment.

It is not established that the consultant failed to read the complete referral. Nor is it established that the Complex Case meeting relied only on the compressed account. Those remain questions for the underlying hospital records. But the documentary loss is real: one version of the clinical task preserved the distinctive foot presentation, and another did not. A system that compresses referrals must have a means of preventing that loss from altering the work performed.

There is a further point. The eventual specialist examination did encounter material capable of restoring what the précis had omitted. The patient was not dependent solely upon whether somebody later retrieved the GP's original wording. The clinician saw a strikingly abnormal gait and described its footdrop-like appearance as suggestive of dystonic posture rather than actual foot drop. Hammertoes and pes planus were recorded. The clinical relationship among foot posture, abnormal movement and pain was therefore recoverable from the admission itself.

The duty arising on acceptance of the referral was to assess the patient with reasonable skill, explain the findings and limits, arrange suitable further investigation or referral for unresolved issues, and communicate a safe handover to the GP and patient. The specialist did not have to discover every answer in eight days. But an unresolved question does not become completed merely because a large number of other investigations have been performed.

03Eight days, one early meeting, and the choice of question

The July 2017 admission was substantial. The patient was 39 years old with a very long history of changing movement and gait abnormalities. Monaghan examined him at length, considered a young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome, ordered investigations, sought colleagues' views and documented unusual features. A DaTscan was arranged and subsequently showed abnormal dopaminergic uptake. The diagnostic work on Parkinsonism was detailed. It was also incomplete in relation to the patient's full lower-limb presentation.

The patient recalls the departmental Complex Case meeting taking place very early in the admission, on the second hospital morning. That precise timing requires confirmation from meeting records. What the later documentary account does establish is that the case was discussed with departmental colleagues and that Professor Timothy J. Counihan advised caution in the commencement and escalation of levodopa in a young patient. In a Medical Council submission dated 29 July 2025, Monaghan described Counihan's involvement as limited to that one early discussion, with no further involvement formally or otherwise in the patient's management.

That statement places an important boundary around the senior consultation. Counihan's advice was real according to the treating consultant. It was not continuing supervision over the next eight years. Nor does the 2025 account show what materials Counihan received, whether he saw the patient in person, or whether the painful feet and toe-clawing were included in the case presented to the meeting. The fact of a case discussion is not proof that every feature of the case received specialist attention.

The departmental expertise was relevant. Counihan and Galway colleagues were associated with contemporary work on dystonic foot presentation in juvenile, PRKN-related Parkinsonism. This does not mean Counihan necessarily recognised PRKN in this patient or that toe-clawing alone established dystonia. It does show that the relationship between early-onset Parkinsonism and abnormal foot posture was a recognisable specialist question within the department, not an obscure association discovered only years later.

The departmental advice that survives in Monaghan's account concerns treatment caution. Young people taking dopaminergic medication may experience dyskinesia and other troublesome complications. That is a legitimate subject for careful clinical judgment. The question is whether the department, in reaching a strategy, adequately defined the condition that strategy was meant to treat. The painful feet could not simply be placed beside the Parkinsonian syndrome as an independent structural or neuropathic matter without sufficient investigation of their relationship.

The potentially decisive sequence is this: an early departmental discussion provides authority for caution with levodopa; the full phenotype is not demonstrably integrated; the consultant then completes an account that presents a coherent diagnostic and therapeutic path. If that is the sequence, the treatment decision may have become authoritative before the diagnostic task was sufficiently complete. The exact meeting chronology and content must be obtained to determine how securely that inference can be made.

04The foot was seen, but its meaning was divided

Monaghan's own examination described a strikingly abnormal gait. The patient walked with an appearance resembling foot drop, which the consultant considered suggestive of dystonic posture, explicitly saying that it was not true foot drop. Elsewhere the account recorded a left-leg kick or foot throw, gross bradykinesia, flat arches and hammertoes. The DaTscan request later described the walk as almost dystonically unusual. These observations were not lost altogether. They were used as neurological evidence in the investigation of unusually early-onset Parkinsonism.

That is the revealing asymmetry. The lower limb was sufficiently neurological to support the principal Parkinsonian investigation. But the pain in the feet attached to that lower limb was given a different explanatory route. The final synthesis suggested that symptoms including corns, calluses and burning might be completely separate or perhaps represent a secondary peripheral neuropathy, possibly related to previous alcohol consumption. The relationship was described as not clear. It also attributed a marked separation between leg tension and painful feet to the patient's own mind.

There were genuine distinctions in the patient's history. He had described differences between leg tension and foot pain, and years of standing work had affected painful pressure lesions. Real structural pathology existed. Those facts deserved attention. They did not independently establish that abnormal neurological posture and movement made no contribution to pain, toe deformity or foot loading. A patient can distinguish sensations without determining their pathophysiological causes.

The word burning is significant because its first identified appearance in the July account sits inside the passage proposing neuropathy; its precise contemporaneous provenance has not been established. The earlier narrative also contains a shift from foot pain to leg pain whose origin needs examination. Neither detail proves fabrication. Each matters because vocabulary can begin organising later reasoning. Once the record introduces neuropathic pain as an explanation, subsequent symptoms, treatment decisions and letters can adopt that language even while objective confirmation remains absent.

The original GP described toe-clawing in bed. The specialist recorded hammertoes but did not visibly analyse whether toe posture was dynamic or fixed, how it related to dystonia, whether it changed with medication, or how it contributed to pain and abnormal loading. The record does not demonstrate a directed integrated distal-foot assessment sufficient to account for all those relationships. This is a documentary finding, not proof that every undocumented manoeuvre was omitted. It is also not a claim that all the patient's pain was caused by dystonia. It is a claim that the relevant distinction needed to be investigated rather than settled by an explanatory branch whose evidential foundation remained thin.

The clinician could have recognised Parkinsonism accurately and still failed to characterise a major part of its expression. This is why the phrase extensive neurological investigation, though incomplete, is the proper description of the admission. The amount of work done cannot answer the separate question of whether the right clinical task was completed.

05Clonazepam, procyclidine, and an early separation of treatment from explanation

During the admission, nocturnal clonazepam was used to relieve leg tension. In the written plan the consultant described the treatment as commenced and considered continuing it long term unless others advised differently. He also contemplated removing it if nocturnal procyclidine became tolerable. The reason for treating the tension was not mysterious: the patient had longstanding symptoms of increased tone, restlessness and uncomfortable muscular activity. The neurological presentation made symptomatic treatment understandable.

But the GP had described toe-clawing in bed as part of the same distressing presentation. The consultant's ability to treat leg tension did not establish that the painful feet were separate from it. The clinical problem called for both symptom relief and examination of the relationship among movement, posture, structural damage and pain. Instead, treatment of tension could proceed as one stream while structural foot care and possible neuropathic pain proceeded as another.

The later procyclidine experience supplied a major opportunity to test that division. In a contemporaneous email of 4 October 2017, the patient described marked improvement after beginning treatment: walking, writing, standing upright and limb looseness improved dramatically. He also described renewed lower-limb and foot phenomena on withdrawal. These were not merely retrospective recollections created for a complaint years later. They were reported at the time.

The response to such observations need not have been an immediate declaration that all foot pain was dystonia. It should have been a serious clinical question: if neurological treatment changes foot behaviour and the patient's wider lower-limb state, what does that say about the original understanding of the feet? Later records show Monaghan recognising medication-sensitive foot phenomena, and in 2020 he documented normal dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion when determining that a footdrop-like movement was Parkinsonian. That was genuine further clinical reasoning.

Yet the record does not demonstrate a corresponding reconstruction of the full painful-foot phenotype. The consultant could revise the explanation of a foot movement without visibly revising the status of the painful feet as a whole. This is an example of local correction that leaves the founding partition intact. The treatment worked on features of the patient, but the explanatory architecture did not necessarily change with it.

The distinction is important because the allegation is not that no one treated the patient. It is that treatment could provide relief and produce new diagnostic evidence while the official understanding remained substantially organised around the original separation. Improvement and deterioration both entered a record that was capable of accommodating them without having to revisit the clinical task that had been left unfinished.

06The bedside protest and the closing account

The patient recalls confronting Monaghan on the morning of 10 July 2017, in the presence of four medical students, before discharge. He says he explained that after eight days the hospital had investigated many things but had not properly investigated his painful feet, the reason he originally sought help. He used the analogy of putting a car through a complete inspection while ignoring bald tyres. This was not a retrospective demand for perfect foresight. It was an immediate complaint that the accepted clinical task had not been completed.

The protest is patient testimony. It is not recorded in the completed hospital narrative, and independent confirmation from students, teaching rosters, contemporaneous records or other witnesses remains to be sought. Its absence from a disputed account is not proof that it did not happen. Equally, the precise order in which the relevant discharge sentences were written, edited and finalised remains unknown. An audit of native document versions could show whether important explanatory wording was already present or was introduced after the alleged protest.

The significance of the protest is independent of a theory of deliberate retaliation. If the patient raised this concern before discharge, the consultant was placed on notice that the painful-foot problem remained unresolved in the patient's experience. A clinically responsive answer could have involved examining the feet again, acknowledging the limits of what had been investigated, obtaining appropriate expertise, or making a specific accountable plan to resolve the question after discharge.

The completed account instead made the patient himself part of the explanation for separation. It recorded that he had markedly divorced leg tension from painful feet in his own mind. This is a consequential attribution. It can make the division appear to come from the patient's own conceptual understanding rather than from the clinician's decision about how the symptoms should be organised. The patient may accurately have described different sensations; that is not the same as asking for them to be treated as neurologically unrelated.

The final note stated that a neuropathic or separate cause was possible and not clear. The plan then incorporated the preceding account. That is where the apparent incompleteness of the clinical task meets the documentary authority of the discharge. A question can remain unresolved in the narrative while nonetheless receiving an operative disposition in the plan. The patient leaves not with a clearly identified unanswered relationship to be examined, but with a signed account in which the uncertain separation has become usable for future care.

If the protest took place as described, it was an early opportunity to prevent the subsequent problem. The institution had not yet committed itself to years of continuity under the completed letter. It could have made the unfinished task explicit. The final record does not show that this happened.

07Genetics and the authority to decide what knowledge matters

The genetics section contains a second route by which an incomplete understanding could become a stable management framework. The consultant asked whether a firm genetic diagnosis would actively change management and wrote that in the majority of cases this was usually answered in the negative. He then specifically entertained PRKN/PARK2, other recessive causes and the possibility that genetic information might illuminate prognosis or levodopa sensitivity. He acknowledged that he was not a movement-disorders neurologist and suggested that testing might be delayed until genetic knowledge and available techniques improved.

The question about management is not illegitimate. Clinicians routinely consider whether a proposed test will alter immediate care. The problem is the order of reasoning. If the complete clinical phenotype has not been adequately characterised, it is premature to treat the existing management strategy as the fixed reference against which the value of further diagnostic knowledge will be judged. Genetic confirmation might not require an immediate different prescription, but it could alter interpretation of young-onset dystonia, counselling, future planning and the confidence attached to earlier assumptions.

The letter also does not identify a single actor who has conclusively decided that this unusual case belongs to the majority for whom genetic clarification would not change management. It invokes broad clinical practice and repeatedly uses a collective we, while the consultant remains responsible for the patient-specific plan. Collegial advice about caution with levodopa is documented, but it does not independently settle the significance of the painful feet. The fact that a consultant acknowledges the limit of his subspecialist expertise makes clear ownership of unresolved questions more, not less, important.

The document says therapy is the most important issue and asks whether the patient can be improved. It then lays out a deliberately conservative strategy toward levodopa and dopamine agonists. It recognises that levodopa might eventually work very well and that delayed introduction might later be regretted. That shows the treatment strategy was not formally irreversible. But no equivalent concrete trigger is documented for reopening the original separation between neurological leg involvement and foot pain.

The patient was informed of the levodopa-caution rationale. The contemporaneous account states that he understood it. That is not identical to a patient having participated in the earlier departmental choice to adopt that strategy. Nor is it identical to having had an open opportunity to challenge the clinical framework within which treatment options were presented. Choices among medicines are not a substitute for a properly explained and revisable understanding of the condition being treated.

In later correspondence Monaghan offered to arrange PRKN testing or a panel if the patient wanted it, while again expressing the view that knowing would not change current management. Professor Daniel Healy's subsequent suspicion of PRKN and support for testing made the issue concrete. Yet molecular confirmation did not occur until 2026. That timing alone does not establish that testing would have prevented the suffering. It does show that the genetic question remained open while the existing management approach continued.

08The signature and “as per above”

The document's key recommendation is short: Plan/Recommendations: As per above. On its face this is a common cross-reference in medical writing. In this case its function requires close attention. It adopts the preceding narrative as the basis of the forward plan. It does not convert every uncertain diagnosis into a proven fact. But it makes the consultant's organisation of the patient's problems the operative structure under which the next clinician, the GP, the patient and the treating service will act.

The signature is equally important. It does not make the account infallible or legally irrevocable. It does make the narrative and recommendations the responsible consultant's authorised account of the admission. The clinician has put professional authority behind the way the information has been assembled, including the distinction attributed to the patient, the uncertain neuropathy branch and the cautious treatment strategy. That account is now available to be quoted, copied, inherited and defended.

The concern is documentary closure without equivalent clinical closure. The original task included a relationship that had not demonstrably been examined to completion. The signed plan did not visibly retain that relationship as an outstanding specialist responsibility. Instead it offered routes through which different elements could be managed: neurologic Parkinsonian treatment, podiatry for physical foot problems and medication or investigation directed toward potential neuropathic pain. Each route could be reasonable by itself. The missing work was integration.

The practical consequence of such a document is not that no clinician can ever change it. It is that later clinicians encounter a patient whose earlier specialist encounter appears to have reached a meaningful endpoint. The apparent authority of a comprehensive specialist account can conceal the difference between uncertainty that remains after appropriate examination and uncertainty arising because a necessary examination was not completed. The phrase not clear may describe either. The document does not itself demonstrate which applied.

Over time, new facts can be placed into the existing categories. A foot movement can be Parkinsonian. Pressure lesions can remain structural. Neuropathic medication can help some pain. Levodopa can improve walking and morning symptoms. None of these observations automatically forces reconsideration of the founding distinction. That is how a framework can be flexible in its treatments but rigid in what it permits itself to understand.

The signed document thus creates a professional commitment in a practical, not contractual, sense. A consultant revisiting the original clinical division later would also be revisiting an explanation he authored, a plan he recommended and subsequent decisions taken under his continuing care. This may create a pressure toward consistency. No claim about his personal pride, hidden motives or an express agreement with governance is needed to identify that risk. A hospital must have systems capable of supporting correction even when correction would require its clinicians to revise their own authoritative accounts.

09The three institutional actors

Three actors appear in the proposed causal account. Monaghan was the responsible treating consultant. Counihan was a senior departmental neurologist with relevant movement-disorders expertise whose advice was invoked in the initial treatment strategy. Galway University Hospital was the organisation that accepted the referral, maintained the information system, authorised clinical practice and provided continuing care and oversight.

These roles must not be collapsed. Counihan's participation in an early Complex Case discussion does not establish that he controlled the eight subsequent years. Monaghan's later Medical Council submission explicitly says Counihan had no further formal or informal involvement in this patient's management. The available records also do not establish that Counihan personally examined the patient during the meeting or received the complete GP referral. His formal status as departmental head at the time needs separate documentary verification; senior clinical expertise and formal governance office are different things.

What can be said is that highly relevant expertise existed in the service. A contemporary Galway-authored clinical reasoning report concerned a dystonic foot in young PRKN-related Parkinsonism. In the patient's own case, the GP reported clawing toes, the treating consultant observed dystonic-looking movement, and PRKN appeared in the genetic differential. This convergence made an integrated foot assessment a serious and foreseeable question, regardless of whether anyone could already prove the genetic subtype.

The department's contribution, as later described, supplied caution about levodopa. The full content of the meeting is not known. It might have addressed the painful feet and left unresolved questions; it might not have been given the distinctive foot information at all. Either outcome would require a different explanation. Meeting minutes, case lists, referral materials and teaching records are therefore central evidence, not administrative trivia.

The hospital's responsibility was wider than the consultant's individual reasoning. It had systems for handling referrals, creating clinical summaries, arranging multidisciplinary advice, tracking care, responding to serious warnings and correcting records. It did not have to review every outpatient letter line by line. But an institution that allows a clinical question to be narrowed by its information system needs a credible method of detecting clinically important losses and returning unresolved work to the correct specialists.

The phrase as per above is not proof that a governance officer ordered or approved the disputed explanation. It is an ordinary documentary phrase carrying a governance consequence within a hospital-controlled process. The signature did not require a secret arrangement to become effective. The hospital's ordinary structure of professional authority was enough to put the written plan into operation. The serious allegation is that this structure may have supplied continuity of authority without an adequate independent means of testing the initial premises.

102018 to 2021: real corrections inside the original frame

The years that followed do not support the claim that the consultant ignored every new development. They show treatment, reassessment and genuine clinical observations. The question is whether those developments were ever joined into a fresh understanding of the complete foot problem.

The 2017 treatment response and withdrawal experience showed that neurological medication materially affected movement and lower-limb function. In 2018 the consultant recorded a foot phenomenon returning as medication was withdrawn. In 2020 he examined distal lower-limb power and identified a footdrop-like movement as Parkinsonian rather than actual weakness. These were opportunities to compare the foot findings at rest, in motion, under treatment and without treatment. The 2020 entry documents a more directed motor assessment than is visible in the original foot disposition.

The record then moves toward dopaminergic treatment. Sinemet was introduced in 2021. This is a real adjustment to the earlier strategy of postponement. It refutes any literal claim that treatment could never change. It does not refute the more serious possibility that the clinician continued changing treatments without fully reopening the original feet–movement separation.

In the same period, a statement about deep brain stimulation described it as helping only tremor. That is not an accurate general description of DBS in Parkinson's disease, where selected patients may receive benefit for other motor complications. The statement does not establish that this patient was suitable for DBS. It is relevant because a patient whose main problem was not tremor could receive an unduly narrow understanding of a specialist treatment avenue. Whether it affected any actual referral decision must be established separately.

Other complications and adverse effects of medication were real considerations. Dyskinesia and dopamine-related complications can constrain management. The point is not that levodopa could have been increased without limit or that every cautious decision was unjustified. The question is whether the full burden of severe foot pain and the evidence of medication-responsive lower-limb dysfunction were weighed inside an adequately reconstructed clinical picture.

During these years, the foot problem continued to move between neurological, structural and neuropathic descriptions. Podiatry treated real corns, calluses and deformities. Neurology adjusted medications. Those approaches could coexist appropriately if somebody retained ownership of the relationship among them. The surviving record is detailed about individual streams of care but less clear about the moment when those streams were brought together to answer the GP's original integrated question.

11The record begins to quote itself

On 14 November 2019, Monaghan opened a letter by explaining that some GPs preferred shorter letters and that previous details were as recorded in older letters already supplied. This apparently mundane practice matters. A later clinical letter can incorporate an earlier account without restating its premises. Repetition need not be literal for the original explanation to retain authority.

Such referencing is ordinarily useful. It avoids unnecessary duplication. But it is dangerous if the earlier account contains an unresolved or insufficiently tested partition of the patient's symptoms. The more often subsequent letters refer back to that history, the more the founding account may acquire the appearance of an established clinical baseline. New observations are then interpreted against it, rather than the original account being tested against the observations.

The 2019 GP material again connected young-onset Parkinson's, painful clawed feet and gait problems. That information could have provided another route to an integrated reassessment. Instead, the longitudinal record displays a recurring division: the painful feet receive structural and neuropathic attention, while Parkinsonism and movement-state problems receive neurological attention. The distinction between appropriate parallel care and an unsafe partition depends on whether someone investigated their relationship.

The document trail also shows why sheer volume is not independent confirmation. Ten letters written by the same clinician may demonstrate close follow-up and continuity, but they do not amount to ten independent tests of the first letter's fundamental assumptions. Later citations to an earlier opinion are descendants of that opinion. They are not necessarily fresh evidence supporting its correctness.

This is the practical form of documentary path dependence. Once a specialist-authored explanation is accepted, it can shape what later clinicians expect, how new symptoms are described, which investigations are requested, which treatments seem appropriate and how disagreements are recorded. A patient can end up repeating the vocabulary of the inherited diagnosis because it is the vocabulary through which care is available. That adoption does not independently verify the original explanation.

By the time of the 2025 complaint, the clinician had eight years of notes with which to explain his decisions. The very duration of that record could appear persuasive. Yet the question was whether the history had been built on an adequate understanding from the beginning. The existence of the later letters could not itself answer that question.

122022 to 2025: the warning and the reversal

The patient's account is that foot pain became almost constantly maximal across late 2022, 2023, 2024 and into 2025. The records contain repeated descriptions of severe painful feet and lower-limb symptoms. They also show structural foot problems, neuropathic-pain treatment, movement fluctuations and medication constraints. Multiple mechanisms could contribute to the experience. The existence of more than one mechanism does not lessen the need to understand how they interact or to respond adequately to the intensity of suffering.

In March 2023, the GP sent an urgent warning describing burning pain in the feet and lower legs, at times associated with a death wish. The GP expressed concern that despite the patient's outward presentation he was not doing well and requested review before the situation became dangerous. This was not a vague hint of discomfort. It was a documented patient-safety signal. The relevant question is what risk assessment, safety planning, clinical reassessment and communication followed. The presently reviewed record does not establish a sufficient answer. It would be inaccurate to say absolutely no clinical action occurred; it would be equally inaccurate to treat the existence of routine follow-up as proof that the warning was managed adequately.

Pregabalin was used for pain, sometimes with benefit but incomplete relief. Its benefit does not prove a peripheral neuropathy diagnosis. Drugs can help symptoms that arise through more than one mechanism. By 2025 the consultant's own wording still allowed neuropathy as conditional: if there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here. Thus, after almost eight years, a central explanatory branch remained a possibility rather than an established diagnosis.

Then Sinemet controlled release produced profound relief. The clinician acknowledged that response in March 2025, recognised the years of pain, and apologised for the patient's experience. The patient describes not receiving a satisfactory direct explanation of what the response meant for the earlier clinical understanding. The consultant's response instead defended gradual levodopa escalation and presented neuropathic-pain treatment as part of an overall strategy to limit dopaminergic exposure.

The dramatic response does not prove that every episode of pain over eight years would have disappeared with earlier Sinemet CR. It does prove the importance of asking what treatment-state responsiveness revealed about the condition, and whether earlier decisions had sufficiently addressed that possibility. Where severe pain was sustained over years, a later major improvement is not only a successful prescription. It is new clinical evidence requiring interpretation against the entire history.

The central problem is the absence of demonstrated fundamental reconstruction. The clinician could acknowledge that the Parkinson's medicine helped the feet while continuing to describe a possible separate neuropathic contribution. That could be clinically right in a mixed condition, but it needed evidential support. The question is whether the neuropathy branch remained operational partly because it had always been present in the clinician's own account, rather than because subsequent objective findings had established it.

132025: the account becomes its own defence

The patient's March 2025 complaint challenged diagnostic anchoring, the treatment history and the failure to revisit the painful-foot presentation. Monaghan responded through the hospital's Quality and Patient Safety process. His answer emphasised the risks of levodopa treatment in younger people, the use of Sinemet since 2021, consideration of other diagnoses and the partial relief of pain by medications including pregabalin. He acknowledged profound relief following Sinemet CR while arguing that the history of cautious management had been reasonable.

The clinician's July 2025 Medical Council submission made the institutional lineage especially visible. He enclosed his longitudinal clinical documents and referred repeatedly to the explanations already supplied to the hospital. He said Counihan had advised at the 2017 Complex Case meeting and had no further part in the patient's management. He maintained that he had always viewed the case as Parkinson's disease with possible neuropathic features rather than an exclusive alcohol-related neuropathy diagnosis.

That defence addressed real elements of the patient's complaint. There had been dopaminergic treatment; the consultant had considered Parkinsonism throughout; neuropathy had not always been stated as proven. But it did not, by those facts alone, demonstrate that the original relationship among painful feet, clawing toes, dystonic-looking gait, abnormal loading and treatment-state effects had been comprehensively examined. A differential can remain formally open while one branch dominates practical management.

The signed institutional outcome dated 1 September 2025 said the Preliminary Assessment Report had been presented to the Serious Incident Management Team on 27 August. It said the multidisciplinary team was reassured by clinical notes showing careful consideration of levodopa risks in a young patient. It noted alternative diagnoses and that Sinemet had been prescribed in 2021. On that basis it communicated a finding of no deficit in care.

That outcome demonstrates what the hospital said. The complete Preliminary Assessment Report, its source schedule and the deliberative record are not presently available here. The signed letter does not demonstrate that the team independently compared the original GP referral with the hospital précis, established the full Complex Case presentation, investigated the alleged bedside protest, tested the provenance of the neuropathy wording, or reconstructed the painful-foot phenotype from the beginning.

The danger is obvious. The institution may have used the existence of a detailed clinical history, and the clinician's recorded caution, as sufficient evidence that the history was appropriately framed. But the patient's complaint concerned whether that frame had been inadequate from the outset. A review cannot fully answer that kind of complaint merely by trusting the documents whose foundational reasoning is disputed.

The consultant's authority to author the account was not the same as independent verification of that account. The length of care was not the same as complete assessment. The appropriateness of one medication decision was not the same as the adequacy of the whole clinical understanding. These distinctions should have structured the institutional investigation.

14What governance was for

Clinical governance exists so that patient care is not dependent solely on whether one clinician happens to reconsider an earlier judgment. Consultants are entitled to exercise professional judgment. Hospitals must provide systems that support safe referral handling, clear allocation of responsibility, competent specialist access, documentation, escalation, review and learning. Governance is not only a complaint-handling office that becomes involved when a patient files a formal grievance. It is part of how clinical authority operates from the moment a hospital accepts a patient.

Here the institution accepted an integrated GP referral, generated a compressed version, facilitated a Complex Case discussion, authorised a consultant-led discharge and maintained eight years of follow-up. It also had access to relevant neurological expertise. These are ordinary institutional functions. The case does not require proof that a governance official personally helped draft the discharge note. The hospital's governance responsibility arises from operating the system in which that note acquired effect.

The central question is whether the system provided an independent route to correction. If an abbreviated referral changed the clinical task, who could detect that? If a patient challenged an incomplete assessment before discharge, who had responsibility to record and respond? If a consultant acknowledged limits to his movement-disorders expertise, who decided whether further specialist assessment was needed? If years of pain continued despite treatment, what triggered review beyond the original consultant? If a GP warned of a death wish, what ensured the warning did not become just another entry in a long chart?

Formal review arrangements need not guarantee that every difficult illness will be diagnosed correctly. They should, however, allow material contradictions to be recognised and examined. The question is not whether Counihan personally should have seen the patient every year. It is whether the service possessed and used an effective mechanism for bringing appropriate expertise to bear once the original unresolved foot problem remained visible over time.

This is also a question of information governance. The original GP referral and compressed referral were different representations of the same clinical task. An authoritative summary can be useful, but there must be accountability for omissions that change clinical meaning. The patient's testimony about the bedside protest and the later challenge to record accuracy further raise the issue of whether the clinical record could accommodate disagreement without simply treating the existing consultant account as definitive.

An actuarial or organisational perspective can create pressures to close episodes, reduce duplication, preserve professional continuity and avoid costly review. Those pressures are plausible in any complex healthcare system. They are not proof of an intentional scheme in this case. The concrete institutional issue is whether the controls designed to protect patients from those pressures functioned when this patient needed them.

The hospital's 2025 decision focused, at least in its communicated summary, on the justification for cautious Sinemet prescribing. That was a narrower question than whether the whole lower-limb phenotype had been correctly framed at the start. If the foundational question was not investigated, the review risked certifying the management strategy rather than testing the clinical premise that made the strategy seem sufficient.

15The finding and the questions that remain

The case presents a coherent and serious causal hypothesis. A GP sent a complex lower-limb presentation to hospital. The hospital's summary omitted important features. The treating neurologist recognised possible dystonic movement and suspected inherited young-onset Parkinsonism. A departmental discussion supplied caution about levodopa, but the available material does not show whether it addressed the complete foot phenotype. The consultant then gave the painful feet a possibly separate explanation, attributed part of the division to the patient, and signed an account whose management recommendations incorporated the preceding reasoning.

That account became the foundation of years of care. Some later events produced genuine clinical corrections, but there is no demonstrated independent reconstruction of the original feet–gait–tone problem. Severe pain, a 2023 safety warning and a major 2025 response to Sinemet CR did not demonstrably cause the hospital to revisit the founding question. The subsequent complaint response relied on the clinician's own longitudinal documentation, and the hospital's communicated review relied on the clinical notes in finding no deficit.

The causal links still require testing. The original referral and abbreviated version should be compared in their native forms. The date, attendance, materials and conclusions of the 2017 Complex Case meeting should be established. It should be determined whether Counihan actually saw the patient and what was communicated about toe-clawing and dystonic-looking gait. The full versions and audit trail of the discharge account should show when its decisive passages were written. The clinical record should be examined for any directed assessment of dynamic versus fixed foot posture, neurological loading, sensory findings and treatment-state effects that has not yet been identified. The handling of the 2023 GP warning and the exact scope of the 2025 Preliminary Assessment Report should be obtained.

These are not objections to the patient's experience. They are the documents and observations that can move the causal explanation from strong coherence toward independent proof. The testimony of the bedside protest is evidence in its own right. It should be investigated, not dismissed because the disputed clinical account fails to mention it. The details of clinician intention or institutional motive are separate questions. A harmful system failure does not need to have been designed in order to have operated.

The deepest issue is the threshold between investigation and management. In July 2017 the hospital had enough information to know that the patient's painful feet and neurological lower-limb abnormality were clinically connected questions, even if the final explanation remained uncertain. The signed discharge account allowed a possible separation to become the practical basis for moving ahead. The consultant could later know more about particular movements and treatment responses than the original frame expressed, yet continue managing within that frame. What was missing was a clear obligation, actively discharged, to bring the new knowledge back against the original premises.

The resulting question for Monaghan is why the relationship between the painful feet and dystonic-looking limb was not visibly resolved or assigned for integrated specialist investigation. The question for Counihan and the department is what was presented at the initial discussion and how relevant expertise could later be re-engaged. The question for Galway University Hospital is what safeguard prevented an incomplete, signed clinical account from becoming its own evidence of completeness—and, if no such safeguard operated, why not.

The patient entered hospital seeking help for painful feet and deteriorating movement. He left with an important Parkinsonian diagnosis, a cautious treatment strategy and a record that allowed his feet to remain on a separate and uncertain explanatory path. He then spent eight years living under the consequences of those decisions. The central inquiry is whether the hospital's ability to preserve and defend its clinical account outlasted its willingness or capacity to test the account against the patient who had to live with it.

That is the cause of the effects proposed here: not one phrase alone, not one drug alone, and not one missed consultation alone, but the conversion of incomplete clinical understanding into authorised continuity without an adequate mechanism of independent correction. The record should be judged against the patient and the original clinical question, not the patient judged against the durability of the record.
